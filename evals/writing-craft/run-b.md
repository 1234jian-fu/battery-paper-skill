# Stack-pressure dependence of the cycling advantage of thin-layer-modified layered-oxide composite cathodes

## Abstract

The cycling benefit of a cathode modification needs to be assessed under the stack pressures at which it is used. Here, we compare layered-oxide composite cathodes with a thin-layer modification (C) and unmodified controls (U) in cells with Li–In counter electrodes at 4 and 12 MPa. After 100 cycles at 25 °C and 0.2 C, mean capacity retention relative to the first discharge was 88% for C and 72% for U at 4 MPa, compared with 90% and 87% at 12 MPa. The difference between the three-cell means therefore narrowed from 16 to 3 percentage points. At 4 MPa, C also exhibited a lower post-cycling cross-sectional pore-area fraction (3% versus 10%) and a lower fitted interface-related resistance at 50% state of charge (80 versus 140 Ω cm²). These endpoint measurements associate the larger low-pressure retention advantage with differences in morphology and impedance, but do not separate geometric-contact and chemical contributions. The comparison shows that the size of the observed mean retention advantage depends on stack pressure.

## Introduction

Here, we test whether the cycling advantage of a thin-layer modification persists when stack pressure increases from 4 to 12 MPa. We compare modified layered-oxide composite cathodes (C) with unmodified controls (U) in cells with Li–In counter electrodes, holding the cathode active-material mass, electrode area and sulfide-electrolyte proportion constant. Comparing both samples at each pressure distinguishes the presence of a mean retention advantage from its sensitivity to pressure. Post-cycling cross-sectional imaging and impedance measurements at 4 MPa then examine how the larger low-pressure advantage relates to morphology and interface-related resistance. This approach identifies the conditions under which the modification provides the larger observed benefit while defining the limits of an interfacial explanation.

## Results

### The retention advantage narrows at higher stack pressure

The modified cathodes showed a larger mean capacity-retention advantage over the unmodified controls at 4 MPa than at 12 MPa (Fig. 1a). In cells with Li–In counter electrodes cycled at 25 °C and 0.2 C, C and U retained 88% and 72%, respectively, of their first-discharge capacities after 100 cycles at 4 MPa. At 12 MPa, the corresponding means were 90% and 87%, narrowing the C–U difference from 16 to 3 percentage points. This narrowing mainly reflected the higher mean retention of U at 12 MPa; C differed by only 2 percentage points between pressures. Each value is the mean of three independent cells for the specified sample and pressure.

At 4 MPa, the larger retention difference was accompanied by a lower pore-area fraction and a lower fitted interface-related resistance for C after cycling. Analysis of four cross-sectional SEM fields from one cell per sample after 100 cycles gave pore-area fractions of 3% for C and 10% for U, using the same segmentation threshold; representative images are shown in Fig. 1b. At 50% state of charge after 100 cycles at 4 MPa, fitting the impedance spectra with the same equivalent circuit gave interface-related resistances of 80 Ω cm² for C and 140 Ω cm² for U (Fig. 1c). These measurements associate the low-pressure retention advantage with differences in the sampled morphology and fitted resistance. Without corresponding pre-cycling pore fractions, the SEM data do not determine how the observed morphological difference developed.

## Discussion

The low-pressure observations are compatible with a contribution from geometric contact to capacity retention: a smaller pore-area fraction accompanies a smaller fitted interface-related resistance in C. This remains an interpretation because the resistance does not separate contact geometry from chemical changes and the images sample only the post-cycling state. Corresponding morphology and impedance measurements at 12 MPa would be needed to test whether the narrower retention gap reflects a smaller difference in interfacial state. The present comparison supports evaluating the modification together with stack pressure, with the numerical advantages treated as descriptive until cell-to-cell variability is available.

## Conclusion

For the tested layered-oxide composite cathodes, thin-layer modification yielded a larger mean retention advantage at 4 MPa than at 12 MPa, while the associated low-pressure morphology and impedance differences leave its interfacial origin unresolved.

## Methods

Layered-oxide composite cathodes with a thin-layer modification (C) were compared with unmodified cathodes (U). The two sample types had the same cathode active-material mass, electrode area and sulfide-electrolyte proportion, with a cathode active-material loading of 4 mg cm⁻². Cells with Li–In counter electrodes were cycled for 100 cycles at 25 °C and 0.2 C using the same voltage window under stack pressures of 4 or 12 MPa. Three independent cells were tested for each sample–pressure combination. Capacity retention was calculated for each cell relative to its first discharge capacity, and the reported values are the means of the three cells. After 100 cycles at 4 MPa, cells were disassembled for cross-sectional SEM. The pore-area fraction for each sample was obtained from four fields of view from one cell, using a common segmentation threshold. Impedance spectra were collected after 100 cycles at 4 MPa at 50% state of charge, over 1 MHz to 10 mHz with a perturbation amplitude of 10 mV. Spectra for C and U were fitted with the same equivalent circuit to obtain the interface-related resistance.
